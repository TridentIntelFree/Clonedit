PADS — tap to play (higher = louder). Tap EDIT then a pad to shape it (gain / pitch / FX). Record pad hits into the grid with the top-right ●, or capture audio to a lane in TRAX.

A pad is a sound plus its settings

Each of the 64 pads (16 at a time, in 4 banks) holds one audio recording and everything about how that recording is played: level, pitch, pan, filter, envelope, and how much reverb and delay it sends. Change a pad's settings and you change every hit of it, everywhere in the song, at once.

Velocity — where you tap matters

Tapping higher up a pad plays it louder. That is velocity, and it is the single biggest difference between a drum part that sounds programmed and one that sounds played. Real drummers do not hit every snare identically. The VEL CURVE in MIDI changes how hard you have to tap to get loud — SOFT makes quiet taps louder, useful on a small screen.

Envelope — ATTACK and RELEASE

ATTACK is how long the sound takes to reach full volume. Zero is instant, which is what you want for drums. Winding it up softens the front of the sound, turning a kick into a swell. RELEASE is how quickly it fades when the note ends — short release makes a tight, clipped sound; long lets it ring.

Choke groups — how real hi-hats work

On a real kit, closing the hi-hat cuts off the open one; they cannot both ring. A CHOKE group does that here. Put two pads in the same choke group and triggering either one silences the other. Classic uses: open and closed hats, or a sample and its own reversed version.

Try this: put your open hat and closed hat on the same choke group, then program them alternating. The part will suddenly sound like one instrument being played rather than two samples overlapping.

Sampling is using a recording as an instrument

Any audio can become a playable sound: a file you import, something you sing into the mic, a note from the built-in synth presets, or a few seconds grabbed from the BLACK BOX (which is always quietly recording the last minute, so you can keep something you did not know you wanted).

Chopping — one recording, many sounds

A chop marks a sample up into slices. The classic use is a drum break: take four bars of a recorded drummer, put a slice point at each hit, then spread those slices across pads with ASSIGN SLICES. Now you can play that drummer's kit in any order you like, at any tempo. That single technique built most of hip-hop and drum & bass.

Speed, pitch, and why they are normally the same thing

Play a recording faster and it also gets higher, the way a tape sped up does — they are the same knob physically. That is often what you want, and SPEED gives it to you. When it is not, KEEP PITCH separates them, so a loop can be stretched to your tempo without turning into chipmunks. It is doing much more work, so extreme settings will sound smeared — that artefact is the cost of the trick, not a bug.

Warping to the tempo

WARP tells the app what tempo a loop was recorded at. Once it knows, the loop can be re-stretched to match your project. Get the source tempo wrong and everything drifts, so it is worth being accurate.

Try this: import any drum loop, CHOP it into 8, then ASSIGN SLICES. Play the pads out of order. You have just made a new break out of someone else's.

PROGRAM — tap steps for the shown TRACK. NOTES opens a scale grid to write melodies on this pad. LIVE REC records pad taps quantized to the grid. LOCK + a step sets pitch / probability / ratchet / nudge. HUMAN adds timing + velocity drift.

Steps are slots in time

A bar of music is divided into 16 equal slots. Each lit step means "play this pad at this moment". Left to right is time; the row you are editing is whichever TRACK is shown. This is the whole idea — everything else is refinement.

Every track can have its own length

Most sequencers give the whole pattern one length. Here each track has its own, which is called polymeter. Set the kick to 16 steps and a shaker to 15 and the shaker slides one step earlier every bar, taking 16 bars to come back around. It is an enormous amount of movement for one setting.

Parameter locks — a different setting on one step

LOCK plus a step changes that single hit without touching the others:

  • PITCH — one note higher or lower. Turns a single sample into a bassline.
  • PROBABILITY — the odds that hit happens at all. At 50% it plays roughly every other pass, so a loop stops repeating exactly.
  • RATCHET — retriggers the hit several times inside its own step. This is the drum-roll and stutter effect.
  • NUDGE — moves the hit slightly early or late, off the grid.
  • VEL — sets that one hit's loudness.

Swing and humanize are not the same thing

SWING delays every second step by a fixed amount. It is a deliberate, repeating feel — the difference between a straight and a shuffled beat. HUMAN adds small random variation in timing and level to every hit instead. Swing is a groove; humanize is the absence of a machine.

Euclidean rhythms

EUCLID spreads a number of hits as evenly as possible across a number of steps. Ask for 5 hits in 16 and you get a pattern found in Cuban, West African and Balkan music alike, because "spread these as evenly as you can" is what people do everywhere. It is a fast way out of a rut.

Try this: program a plain 4-on-the-floor kick, then put one 60% probability lock on the last step. The loop stops sounding looped immediately.

Reading the circle

Outer ring = the selected track — tap its segments to place hits. Inner rings are the other tracks; tap one to select it. Each ring turns at its own LEN, so polymeter is something you can watch.

A circle suits a loop better than a line does, because a loop has no end. The ring turns once per cycle, so the shape you see is the rhythm — evenly spaced hits look evenly spaced.

It earns its place when tracks have different lengths. Two rings turning at different rates visibly drift against each other, and you can watch the point where they come back into alignment. On a grid that relationship is invisible.

Giving a pad its own BPM

Every pad normally plays on the project's beat. Switch one to OWN BPM and it runs at whatever tempo you give it, while everything else carries on as before. Two pulses at once — a polyrhythm.

The number means what you think it means

The project's BPM counts beats per minute. A pad's BPM counts its own hits per minute. So a pad set to the same number as the project hits once per beat, and everything else follows from there:

  • Same as the project — one hit per beat.
  • Double — eighths. Four times — sixteenths.
  • Three times — triplets, three per beat.
  • Three quarters — three hits per bar against four beats. This is the classic 3-against-4, and it is the one you cannot write on the step grid at all: a bar is 16 steps and 16 does not divide by 3.

The QUICK buttons are those relationships, so you can hear them before you work out the arithmetic.

LOCK TO BAR

On, the pad's tempo is nudged to the nearest rate that fits a whole number of hits into a bar, so it returns to the downbeat every time and stays that way forever. This is what you want for a tuplet. The panel shows the tempo it actually settled on.

Off, the pad runs at exactly the BPM you set. Unless that happens to divide the bar evenly it will never come back into line — it drifts through every relationship with the beat and keeps going. That is called phasing, it is a technique rather than a fault, and Steve Reich built whole pieces out of it. Because it never repeats, a drifting pad is cut wherever the bounce ends rather than finishing a cycle.

CELLS

How many cells the row has before it starts again — separate from how fast they go by. Three hits per bar with a five-cell row gives you a rhythm that takes five hits to come round and a bar to line up, which is polyrhythm and polymeter at the same time.

Nested tuplets

Use LOCK ▸ RATCHET on one cell. A cell of a three-per-bar pad ratcheted by five is five hits inside one third of a bar — a quintuplet nested in a triplet. Ratchets subdivide this pad's own pulse, so they nest as deep as you stack them.

Two things it deliberately does not do

Swing is not applied to a pad on its own BPM: delaying every second hit of a triplet is not a shuffle, it is a worse rhythm. REV plays it forwards — a reversed tuplet is not a meaningful thing to want, and faking one would put a difference between what you hear and what the bounce contains.

Try this: put a kick on the ordinary grid at 0, 4, 8 and 12. Then pick a shaker, switch it to OWN BPM, and tap 3:4. They should collide on the downbeat and nowhere else.

Velocity, and why it matters

VEL sets the loudness of steps you place next (step brightness shows it). To change a placed step: LOCK → tap the step → VEL slider.

Velocity is how hard a hit is played. Programming every hit at the same level is the single most recognisable sign of a machine — a real player accents some notes and ghosts others without thinking about it.

The usual pattern is simple: emphasise the beats you would count out loud, and drop everything between them well down. On hats especially, a loud-quiet alternation does more for a groove than any effect.

Notes and chords

Rows are scale notes for THIS pad (root = the sample's own pitch). Tap to place, tap again to remove. Stack several notes in the same column for a chord — they fire together as harmony. Written as pitch locks, so chords bounce + export to MIDI.

A chord is several notes sounding together. The common three-note kind takes a starting note, skips one scale step, takes the next, skips again, takes the next — the 1st, 3rd and 5th degrees. Because the grid rows are already scale notes, stacking every other row gives you a chord that fits the key automatically.

Major and minor differ by one note, the middle one, moved a semitone. That single note is most of the difference between music that sounds bright and music that sounds sad.

These are stored as pitch locks on the step, which is why chords survive into the bounce and export as real chords in the MIDI file.

What the silencer is for

Left = abrupt CUT, right = soft fade. Cuts pads + live instruments and chokes the reverb/delay tails; sound returns on the next programmed or played hit. Bakes into the bounce. (TRAX tape lanes keep rolling.)

Nearly every dance record is built on taking things away. A breakdown is not new material, it is the same loop with most of it removed, and it works because the return feels enormous by contrast.

The hard cut is a rhythmic device — a single silent beat before a drop is one of the oldest tricks there is. The soft fade is for transitions between sections. Choking the reverb tail matters: letting a big reverb ring on through a silence gives the gap away.

Two different kinds of track

The sequencer stores instructions — "play pad 3 here". A tape lane stores audio, exactly as it sounded. That difference decides which one to use. Instructions stay editable forever: change the sample and every hit changes. Audio is fixed once recorded, but it captures things instructions cannot — a vocal, a guitar, a live performance with all its imperfection.

The three sources

  • MASTER BUS — everything you can hear, mixed together. Use it to bounce a section down, or to capture a live tweak of the filters.
  • LIVE ONLY — just what you play: pads you hit by hand, the on-screen instrument, a guitar through AMP. The sequencer keeps playing so you have something to play along to, but it does not end up in the recording. This is how you overdub.
  • MIC — the microphone alone.

Why overdubbing matters

Recording one part at a time while hearing the others is how records have been made since the 1950s. LIVE ONLY is that, on a phone. Each pass adds a layer, and because the backing is silent in the recording, the layers stay independent — you can mute or redo any one of them.

Try this: set a lane to LIVE ONLY, arm it, press PLAY, and hum a bassline into the mic over your beat. Then set the lane to loop with the ∞ button.

Why phone recordings sound thin

A phone speaker produces almost nothing below about 500 Hz. A man's voice has its fundamental around 85–180 Hz and a woman's around 165–255 Hz, so on a phone you are not hearing the fundamental at all — only its harmonics. That is why a take that sounded fine while you recorded it turns distant on playback.

What the two processors do about it

PRESENCE lifts around 3 kHz, where consonants live and where a small speaker is actually efficient. It buys more intelligibility per decibel than anything else available. LIFT works on the low end instead, so the voice keeps its body on speakers that can reproduce it.

Monitoring and feedback

If the mic can hear the speaker, the speaker feeds the mic, which feeds the speaker: that is the squeal. Use headphones before turning MONITOR on. There is no software fix for a loop that exists in the room.

Gain before anything else

Set the input level so the loudest thing you do reaches most of the meter without hitting the top. Too quiet and you amplify hiss later; too loud and the recording is clipped, which cannot be undone. This is called gain staging, and it matters more than any effect.

Try this: record the same line twice, once with PRESENCE flat and once lifted, and listen on the phone speaker. The difference is much larger than it looks on the slider.

The missing fundamental

For playback on a phone. PRESENCE lifts around 3kHz, where consonants live and where a small speaker actually works. BODY builds harmonics of the low fundamental a phone speaker cannot reproduce at all, and lets your ear put the missing pitch back — weight without bass the speaker would swallow.

BODY works on a genuine quirk of hearing. Given the harmonics of a low note but not the note itself, the ear reconstructs the pitch that should be underneath them — you hear a bass note that is not physically present. It is why a phone speaker with no low-end response can still convey a bassline at all.

So rather than boosting frequencies a small speaker cannot produce, which only wastes headroom, BODY generates the harmonics that let your ear supply the missing pitch itself. Weight without bass the speaker would swallow.

What an amp simulator is doing

An electric guitar plugged straight into a recorder sounds thin and brittle, because half of an electric guitar's sound is the amplifier and speaker it is normally played through. The amp section models that: DRIVE is the overload that produces distortion, TONE is the amp's own EQ, and the cabinet colouring is what a speaker in a wooden box does to the top end.

Distortion is a gain problem, not an effect

Turning DRIVE up makes the signal louder before it is clipped, and the clipping is the sound. That means drive and level interact: as you add dirt you usually need to pull the output down to keep the mix balanced.

Headphones, seriously

A guitar pickup near a loudspeaker playing that same guitar is a feedback loop. It can be a musical technique deliberately; accidentally, it is a shriek at full volume.

Try this: record the same riff clean to one tape lane and driven to another, then blend them. Layering a clean track under a dirty one is how most recorded guitar keeps its definition.

Mixing is mostly just level

Before any effect, mixing is deciding how loud each thing is relative to everything else. Most amateur mixes have too much of everything at once. The fastest route to a mix that sounds finished is pulling things down, not adding.

Headroom, and why you leave some

If everything is at maximum, the total is far past maximum, and the result clips. Leaving space — headroom — means the peaks have somewhere to go. If the master is working hard, turn the loud channels down rather than turning the master down; the balance is what needs fixing.

Sends versus per-channel effects

This is the concept worth learning here. The EQ on a channel affects that channel only. REV and DLY are sends: they take a copy of the channel and feed it into one shared reverb and one shared delay. Every pad sends into the same two effects.

That is why they behave the way they do. Change the reverb size and every pad sending to it changes together, which is exactly what you want — a mix sounds like one place when everything shares a room. It is also why a pad can sound like it has an echo you cannot find on the pad itself: the repeat is being made at the master, not on the channel. A pad that is feeding a send says so on its face.

Mute and solo

M silences a channel, S silences everything else. Solo is for listening, not for mixing — decisions made in solo often fall apart in the mix, because instruments mask each other and that masking is real.

Sidechain — the pumping sound

Sidechain ducking turns one sound down whenever another one plays, most often pulling the bass down on every kick. It started as a practical fix (kick and bass fight for the same low frequencies) and became a deliberate effect. Depth sets how far it ducks; release sets how quickly it comes back.

Try this: solo nothing, set every pad to a sensible level with sends at zero, and only then add reverb. Building the balance dry first is the habit that separates fast mixes from long ones.

This is the distinction worth learning. A channel's EQ affects that channel alone. REV and DLY are sends: they take a copy of the channel and feed one shared reverb and one shared delay that every pad feeds.

That is why changing the reverb size changes every pad at once — which is what you want, since a mix sounds like one place when everything shares a room. It is also why a pad can seem to have an echo you cannot find on the pad itself: the repeat is being made at the master, not on the channel.

Width and mono compatibility

WIDTH pushes the stereo image out. It sounds impressive on headphones and can betray you: many places music gets played — phone speakers, clubs, some Bluetooth speakers — collapse stereo to mono, and heavily widened material can partly cancel itself when that happens. MONO lets you check. If the mix loses its bass or an instrument disappears, back the width off.

LUFS is loudness, not peak level

Peak meters show the highest single sample. LUFS measures how loud something actually sounds over time, weighted for human hearing. The two are very different: a heavily compressed track and a dynamic one can share a peak level and be worlds apart in loudness.

This matters because streaming services normalise. They aim for roughly −14 LUFS and turn everything else up or down to match. Master louder than that and it gets turned down — so crushing your mix to be loud buys nothing on Spotify or YouTube and costs you all the dynamics. The readout shows M (the last 400 ms) and S (the last 3 seconds); the bounce reports the integrated figure for the whole track.

True peak, and why the ceiling is not enough

Between any two samples the actual waveform can rise higher than either of them. When a file is converted to MP3 or AAC that in-between level becomes real and can clip, even though every sample looked legal. That is true peak, measured in dBTP, and it is why masters are usually kept about a decibel below maximum rather than right at it.

The limiter is not a brick wall

CEILING is a target, held by a limiter with a 20:1 ratio rather than an absolute barrier, so a little gets through — the bounce report says by how much instead of quietly rounding it off. If the limiter is doing several decibels of work all the time, the fix is in the mix, not here.

Try this: bounce once, read the integrated LUFS in the PROJ log, then compare it with a commercial track you like. If yours is far louder, you are probably losing more than you gain.

Width, and why bass is centred

100% width is untouched; 0% is mono. BASS MONO centres everything below the chosen frequency — wide bass smears on a big system and thins out on a phone speaker. 120Hz is a safe habit.

Widening works by increasing the difference between the two channels. When something plays back in mono those channels are added together, and whatever was different between them partly cancels — so a heavily widened mix can lose material entirely on a phone speaker, a club system or a Bluetooth speaker. Check in mono before trusting a wide mix.

Bass is the worst case, and the reason is physical as well as electrical: on vinyl, out-of-phase low frequencies move the cutting stylus vertically and can throw the needle out of the groove. Centring the low end has been standard practice since long before anything was digital.

Ceiling, limiting and true peak

The limiter grips here, so pushing gain makes the mix louder instead of breaking it, and a soft clipper after it means the output can never reach 0dBFS whatever you do. Anything already below this level is left alone. −1dB is a good default; −0.3dB is as close to the edge as is sensible for a file that will be converted to MP3 or AAC later.

A limiter is a compressor with a very high ratio: below the threshold it does nothing, above it the signal is held back hard. It lets you raise the whole mix without the peaks breaking through, which is how masters get loud.

The reason to stop short of 0dBFS is true peak. Between any two samples the real waveform can rise higher than either of them, and converting to MP3 or AAC turns that in-between level into something real that can clip — even though every sample in your file looked legal. A decibel of margin costs nothing audible and removes the problem.

If the limiter is working hard all the time, the fix is in the mix rather than here. A limiter catching occasional peaks is mastering; a limiter holding down everything is a balance problem with a lid on it.

What AUTO measures

AUTO listens to the whole arrangement — the song if you have one, else the chain, else this pattern — at its loudest possible pass, and sets TRIM so that peak lands on the ceiling. Nothing clips and the limiter is left with nothing to do.

It measures the loudest pass deliberately, not an average and not the section you happen to be hearing. A trim set from a quiet intro would clip the moment everything came in, so it finds the worst case first and works back from there.

It is setting level, not tone or balance. If it pulls things a long way down, that is the arrangement telling you the loudest passage is much louder than the rest — worth fixing in MIX rather than accepting here.

MIDI is instructions, not sound

A MIDI cable carries no audio whatsoever. It carries messages: "note 60 started, fairly hard", "note 60 stopped", "knob 74 is now at 90". The sound is made entirely at the receiving end. That is why the same MIDI part can play a piano or a drum kit, and why a MIDI file is a few kilobytes.

Notes, velocity and channels

Every note has a number (60 is middle C) and a velocity from 1 to 127 saying how hard it was played. Messages also carry a channel, 1 to 16, so several instruments can share one cable and each listen only to its own. OMNI means listen to all of them — the right setting until something clashes.

Two ways to play the pads

Normally each pad answers to one note number, which is how drum kits work. Turn on CHROMATIC and the whole keyboard plays the selected pad instead, transposed — so a single sample becomes a playable instrument across the keys.

CC — the knobs

Continuous controllers are numbered knobs and sliders. LEARN is the easy way to use them: arm it, choose what you want controlled, then move a physical knob. Whichever CC that knob sends is now bound to it. No manuals, no numbers to look up.

Clock — keeping two machines in time

MIDI clock is a steady tick, 24 per quarter note, that lets separate devices agree on tempo. One machine leads and the others follow. CLOCK OUT makes this app the leader; EXT CLOCK makes it a follower, taking its tempo from whatever is sending. Exactly one device in a setup should be the leader — two leaders is the most common reason a rig will not lock up.

Try this: with a controller connected, turn on LEARN, select master volume, and wiggle any knob. It is bound in one move.

Three different kinds of safety net

The app saves the current session automatically a second or so after any edit, so closing the tab does not lose work. Separately, it takes a checkpoint every few minutes, which REWIND can return to — and rewinding saves the present first, so you can always rewind the rewind. Neither of those is the same as a project, which is a named thing you chose to keep. Use SAVE AS NEW before big changes; it costs nothing.

What a bounce is

Bouncing renders the sequence to an audio file, faster than real time, the same way every time. It is the point at which your arrangement of instructions becomes something anyone can play. Whatever the OUT section is doing is included, so the file matches what you were hearing.

Stems, and why anyone wants them

A stem export writes each pad to its own file instead of one mixed track. That is what you hand to someone who is going to mix or remix your material — they get the parts separately and can rebalance anything. It is also a good archive: stems still open in twenty years, in any program, whatever happens to this app.

MIDI export

Exporting MIDI writes out the notes, not the sound, so your pattern can be opened in any other music program and played with completely different instruments. Useful when an idea outgrows the tools it was written on.

The take

Humanize and probability are random, which would normally mean a bounce sounds slightly different every time. The performance is tied to a stored seed instead, so the same project bounces the same take — and NEW TAKE deliberately rolls a different one when you want an alternative. The seed is saved with the project, so it survives reloading.

Try this: bounce, then hit NEW TAKE and bounce again. Same notes, different performance. Keep whichever you prefer.

How AI JAM works

SPEC puts a ready-to-paste prompt (with a working example) in the box — hand it to an AI, paste the reply back, LOAD. The loader pulls the JSON out even if the AI wraps it in ```json or adds chatter. EXPORT lets an AI remix what you made. Console: window.JBH.load / .export / .play / .spec

There is no AI inside this app and no server behind it. AI JAM is a document format: SPEC writes out a prompt describing that format, you hand it to whatever assistant you like, and paste the reply back. The app renders every sound from the description.

Which means it keeps working offline for everything except the part where you talk to an assistant, it sends nothing anywhere on its own, and it is not tied to any particular AI outliving any other.
